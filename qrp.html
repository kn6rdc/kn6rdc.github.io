QRP

QRP is the part of amateur radio that I find most rewarding. There is something satisfying about making a contact with only a few watts and a relatively simple station.

For me, QRP isn't primarily about proving how little power I can use. It's about keeping the station simple, paying attention to propagation and antennas, and actually enjoying the contact.

Why Low Power?

A small transceiver and a modest antenna can do far more than their size would suggest. QRP encourages listening, patience, and learning how radio actually works.

It also makes portable operation much easier. A station that can run from a small battery and fit into a backpack is a station that is easy to take somewhere and use.

My Approach

I generally prefer simple equipment and straightforward operation rather than constantly chasing the latest gear.

The goal is to spend more time operating and less time thinking about what equipment I might buy next.

QRP Philosophy

I enjoy QRP not just as a way to operate at low power, but as a broader approach to amateur radio. To me, it represents a particular mindset and a different way of enjoying the hobby.

A Challenge

One of the things I enjoy most about QRP is the challenge it brings to something that could otherwise become a bit routine. After briefly experimenting with power levels up to 100 watts, I noticed that I enjoyed my QSOs less. Knowing I was operating much like everyone else took away some of the excitement.

I wanted something different: a way to make radio more challenging and engaging. I wanted to introduce some resistance, make success a little less certain, and have to work for each contact. For me, QRP provides that challenge.

Learning CW

After earning my Technician license in 2021, I had fairly limited band privileges. I began activating SOTA summits with nothing more than a handheld radio (HT). After a few activations, I realized this approach could be difficult, especially in more remote areas.

I had two options. I could buy a 100-watt radio, a large battery to power it in the field, and operate SSB. Or I could choose a more portable setup: a simple, three-band, CW-only QRP radio, a wire antenna, and a small battery. T hat second option also meant learning Morse code.

Long story short, I chose the more portable setup and began learning CW.

I signed up for CW Ops CW Academy and never looked back. CW quickly became my preferred operating mode, and it remains so today.

Over the years, I have briefly experimented with higher power, including the 10–15 watt range and the 50-100 watt range. Those QSOs are not included in the numbers above.

I don't regret experimenting with higher power. In fact, it made me realize that the grass really isn't greener on the other side. Running QRP is simply more fun!
